I grew up in the Atlanta suburbs. Sometime around 2014, I noticed that an apartment complex I had passed regularly since I was a child was being torn down. When I dug into it, I found that this was one instance of a region-wide phenomenon. City councils across Atlanta&rsquo;s northern suburbs were approving plans to raze older residences to make way for upscale &ldquo;in-town&rdquo; communities. These developments often followed New Urbanist design principles, and local officials championed them as a way to reverse the suburban disinvestment hastened by the Great Recession. Central to these efforts was the destruction of thousands of relatively affordable apartment units that housed a large share of the region&rsquo;s Hispanic population. The economic development plan, it would seem, was to replace one set of people with another. In the prescient words of Neil Smith, suburban municipal governments across the region appeared to be carrying out a [&ldquo;social cleansing strategy.&rdquo;](https://www.taylorfrancis.com/chapters/edit/10.4324/9780429242670-9/postfascist-sub-urbanism-scott-markley-coleman-allums)

Of the more than 9,000 people living in the 18 complexes that were bulldozed, 59 percent were Hispanic and 29 percent were Black, in a region where Hispanic and Black residents each made up less than 20 percent of the population at the time.
